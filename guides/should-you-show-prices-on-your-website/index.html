Should you show prices on your website?

A plain guide for trades, clinics and local businesses: why customers want a price first, the common worries, and how to show guide prices without boxing yourself in.

By Liam Hockley, AiEngineered. Updated 24 October 2026.

Short answer: for most small businesses, yes, as a guide range rather than a fixed price. A range answers the first question most customers have (“roughly how much?”), quietly filters out people whose budget is nowhere near, and still leaves you free to confirm the exact price once you know the details.

Why customers want a price first

Most people start by searching or asking around, and they want to know whether a job is a £500 job or a £5,000 job before they spend time on calls or home visits. If your website doesn’t say, many will move on to a business that does, or send a vague “how much for…” message that takes you time to answer.

The usual worries, answered

WorryWhat actually helps
“Every job is different.”Show a range for a typical job, and say what moves the price (size, materials, access, urgency).
“I’ll scare people off.”A realistic range mostly removes people who would never have gone ahead, which saves you the visit.
“Competitors will copy me.”They can get your prices by asking for a quote anyway. Customers reward the business that answers clearly.
“People will hold me to it.”Label it clearly as a guide and confirm the final price in writing before work starts.

Guide range or fixed price?

Fixed prices work for standard, repeatable services (a boiler service, a dog groom for a given size, a cut and blow dry). Ranges work better for anything that depends on the job (a roof repair, an extension, a set of veneers). Many businesses use both: fixed prices for small standard jobs and ranges for everything else.

How to word a guide price

  • Say what it covers: “Roof repairs, typical house: £410 to £540.”
  • Say what changes it: “Final price depends on access, materials and what we find.”
  • Say what happens next: “We confirm an exact quote once we’ve seen the job.”
  • If you are VAT registered, show prices to the public including VAT.

What to avoid

  • A “from” price you almost never actually charge. Customers notice, and it undermines trust.
  • Out-of-date prices. Review them when your costs change.
  • Hiding the catch in small print. If emergency call-outs cost extra, say so next to the price.

Common questions

Will competitors see my prices?

They can, but they can usually find out anyway by asking for a quote. Customers comparing businesses are more likely to contact the one that gave them a straight answer.

Do I have to stick to the guide price?

No, as long as it is clearly labelled as a guide and you explain what affects the final price. Keep your guide ranges realistic so they match what you actually charge for typical jobs.
